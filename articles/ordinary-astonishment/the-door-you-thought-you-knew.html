Ordinary Astonishment

The Door You Thought You Knew

On familiarity, déjà vu, jamais vu, and the strange distance between knowing something and feeling that we know it.

By Lizzie I Bee

Door.

There is nothing difficult about the word. Four letters, one syllable, an object so ordinary that you may walk through a dozen of them today without once truly seeing one. Write it down.

Door.

Now again. And again.

Door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door door.

Keep going, and somewhere past the twentieth or thirtieth repetition, something odd tends to happen. The letters start to look wrong, the two o's excessive, almost invented, and the word slides loose from the object it names. You know exactly what a door is. You know exactly that this is the word for one. And yet, for a few seconds, the familiar word begins to look like gobbledygook, or something written in a language you don't recognise.

This is not a private glitch, and you did not imagine it. It has a name, a published study and, improbably, an Ig Nobel Prize. In 2020, psychologist Chris Moulin and his colleagues at the universities of Grenoble and St Andrews asked volunteers to copy familiar words over and over until they felt something "peculiar," or simply couldn't go on. One of the words they chose, for reasons that now feel almost too apt, was door. About two-thirds of participants reported a strange sensation on at least one trial, usually arriving after around thirty repetitions, roughly a minute of writing the same four letters.

The researchers had a name ready for what they had triggered: jamais vu. Never seen. It is the quieter, less famous relative of déjà vu, already seen. Déjà vu hands us a room we have, by any ordinary account, never entered, insisting all the same: I have been here before.

Researchers generally describe déjà vu in terms of false familiarity, the feeling that a present experience has happened before despite there being no corresponding memory that accounts for it. Science can investigate when that feeling occurs, what accompanies it and how it relates to memory and recognition. What science cannot currently tell us is everything that a subjective experience might mean. There is room to wonder about strange experiences without pretending that wondering is evidence, or that an unanswered philosophical question has become a scientific explanation.

Jamais vu runs the more familiar trick in reverse. Something known, held under the light a little too long, suddenly feels unfamiliar. And it isn't confined to the laboratory, or to words. Musicians occasionally lose their place inside a passage they could once have played half asleep. A face you have looked at every day for years can, for a disorienting second, become strangely unfamiliar. You can turn onto a street you have walked a thousand times and feel, briefly, lost.

Hiding inside both of these small malfunctions, false recognition and false alienation, is something so ordinary we almost never examine it: familiarity itself.

The invisible feeling

We talk about familiarity as though it belongs to things. A familiar face. A familiar street. A familiar voice. A familiar song.

I'm drawn to familiarity as a subject partly because it seems disappointingly simple right up until you start pulling on it, and then the whole thing comes apart in your hands.

Where, exactly, does familiarity live?

A childhood home may contain countless things that help you recognise it. There might be a crack in a wall that you remember making with a ball, a mark left by your hand years ago, an uneven step you tripped over as a child, or something your father built that you still recognise immediately. Those things really do belong to the place, and they may make it unmistakably yours. But the feeling that rises when you encounter them, that immediate sense of I know this place, does not live inside the bricks.

The recording of a song may remain exactly the same after the hundredth listen, but your experience of hearing it does not. Familiarity is happening somewhere in the encounter between what is there and what you bring to it, and even that is less simple than it sounds, because familiarity isn't quite the same thing as remembering.

Picture seeing someone in a supermarket and feeling the immediate, wordless certainty: I know that face. You don't know from where, so you start searching. School? An old job? A neighbour, years ago? Someone from television? The recognition arrives first, whole and confident, and the explanation has to run to catch up with it. Sometimes it never does.

Different aspects of knowing can also come apart. Research involving people with Capgras delusion provides a particularly striking example. People can consciously recognise a familiar face while failing to show the usual autonomic response that distinguishes familiar from unfamiliar faces. Recognition and the bodily response associated with familiarity, in other words, need not always travel together.

Memory, recognition and the felt sense of knowing overlap so thoroughly in ordinary life that they can seem indivisible. But they are not identical, nor are they the only processes involved in knowing. Usually everything works together so seamlessly that we have little reason to notice the distinctions. We notice the machinery when something slips.

That is part of what makes déjà vu so unsettling. The feeling insists, This has happened before, while another part of you answers, No, it hasn't. Somehow you can experience the conviction and doubt it at the same time.

Which leaves an uncomfortable question sitting on the table. How much authority should a feeling of knowing be given when the feeling and the fact can come apart?

The things we know because we know them

There are things you almost certainly believe that you cannot remember learning. That there are seven days in a week. That Paris is in France. That you should look before crossing a road. Maybe also that certain foods are healthy, certain neighbourhoods dangerous, certain kinds of people trustworthy, or certain historical events happened more or less the way you were told.

Some of those beliefs are demonstrably true. Some may not be. But true and merely familiar can acquire the same comfortable texture: of course.

That phrase is worth pausing on. Of course is what we say when something no longer seems to require checking, and repetition is remarkably good at contributing to that feeling whether or not the thing being repeated is true.

Psychologists call this the illusory truth effect: the well-documented finding that repeated exposure to a claim can make people somewhat more likely to judge it as true, even when repetition itself provides no evidence for the claim. The effect has limits. People do not believe everything they hear twice. Existing knowledge, plausibility, context and scepticism all matter. But repetition can still influence judgments of truth.

One explanation involves what researchers call processing fluency. Something we have encountered before is often easier for the mind to process, and that ease can influence how credible it feels.

That would be a modest curiosity in a world where information arrived occasionally, perhaps in a letter once a month. We do not live in that world.

The room with a thousand doors

A claim appears on your phone. You scroll past it. Later, someone repeats it in a video. A screenshot of the video turns up somewhere else. A commentator discusses the screenshot. Someone you trust shares the commentary. A friend mentions it, in passing, over dinner.

Six encounters. But how many sources?

Perhaps six. Perhaps one, wearing six different coats.

The distinction can become genuinely difficult to see from inside the scroll. Information moves through networks where repetition can look like corroboration, and where a single claim can pass through a thousand doors and arrive each time looking like a fresh visitor. Research into repeated misinformation gives us reason to take that repetition seriously. Encountering misinformation more than once can increase its perceived accuracy and can even influence people's willingness to share it.

Something begins to feel as though it is everywhere. And maybe it is. But everywhere is not the same thing as independently checked.

The systems through which information reaches us were built for many different purposes, and it would be too simple to pretend that every algorithm operates in the same way. But on major social platforms, keeping people watching, clicking, reacting and returning is an important part of the system. What succeeds at capturing attention and what deserves our confidence are not necessarily the same thing.

This makes familiarity consequential socially and politically. Yet, almost as a side effect, our strange information networks have also given us something rarer and more hopeful. The unfamiliar has started walking through our doors too.

For most of human history, enormous stretches of humanity remained almost entirely imaginary to one another. You might have had a word for people living thousands of kilometres away, and stories about them, and a government or newspaper only too willing to tell you what "those people" were like. But you were unlikely ever to watch one of them make breakfast.

Now you can. You can watch a Palestinian father teasing his daughter, an Iranian teenager complaining about homework, a Ukrainian grandmother planting tomatoes, or a South African arguing about rugby with an Australian. You can hear someone laugh before you understand a single word they are saying, and something in you understands anyway. People who once arrived only as categories can now arrive as individuals, mid-sentence, mid-laugh, entirely themselves.

Research into intergroup contact suggests that even mediated, screen-shaped encounters can sometimes soften attitudes toward people perceived as belonging to another group. It isn't automatic. What we see, how it is framed and what we already believed before encountering it all matter enormously. A screen can humanise people we have previously known only as categories, but it can also caricature them or repeatedly show us one distorted fragment until we mistake that fragment for the whole.

Even when what we are seeing is real, it is still a selected view. Someone chose what to record and what to share. Editors, broadcasters, governments, content creators and platforms may all influence what eventually reaches us, while algorithms can influence which fragments we encounter repeatedly. We bring our own assumptions to the other side of that exchange too. A glimpse into somebody else's life can be genuine without being complete.

When strangers become familiar

Perhaps one of the strangest consequences of seeing more of the world is discovering how ordinary everyone else turns out to be. People elsewhere make jokes that don't land. They worry about money at two in the morning. They get irritated with relatives at holidays. They fall for unsuitable people. They sing badly in the car. They lose their keys.

This shouldn't come as news to anyone, and yet historically it very often has. Distance makes abstraction easy. Familiarity makes it harder to sustain. It is simple to speak casually about "a population." It becomes more difficult once you have seen inside someone's kitchen.

Different religion, different politics, different language, and underneath those differences are experiences that require little translation: loving a child, grieving someone who has died, worrying about money, hoping somebody you love gets home safely, wanting to belong somewhere.

And perhaps the circle extends beyond us.

Elephants have been observed showing particular and sustained interest in the bodies and bones of dead members of their species. In 2018, an orca known as Tahlequah carried her dead calf for at least seventeen days and roughly a thousand miles. Scientists are appropriately cautious about claiming to know precisely what another species experiences internally, and behaviour cannot give us direct access to another mind.

But this is where the evidence ends and my own interpretation begins. I find it difficult to look at an animal remaining with the body of her dead calf for seventeen days and regard the word grief as an unreasonable human projection. Perhaps our mistake is sometimes not that we see too much of ourselves in other animals, but that we have spent too long insisting that experiences we recognise in ourselves must belong uniquely to us. I cannot prove that Tahlequah's grief felt like mine, or that an elephant touching the bones of another understands death as I do. I don't need them to be the same. To me, the possibility that grief reaches beyond our species does not diminish what is human about it. It makes grief older, stranger and more universal, part of the deep history of attachment between living things.

There is a trap folded into this too. Discovering that one story you were told was incomplete does not automatically make the next story complete, and realising that something familiar was false does not make whatever feels new and unfamiliar true. The mind can trade one confident certainty for another with no pause in between, no wiser for the exchange.

A door opens, we walk through it, and promptly forget that the new room has doors of its own, leading somewhere we haven't yet thought to check.

When the known becomes strange

There is another kind of unfamiliarity, one with little to do with laboratory word-copying but that feels strangely related to it: the moment something you have known for years quietly stops looking the way it used to.

Anyone who has spent an hour editing a paragraph knows the feeling. Somewhere around the fiftieth rereading, ordinary words start looking as though somebody made them up five minutes ago. I've had perfectly ordinary sentences suddenly strike me as suspicious, as if they'd slipped into the piece from somewhere else while I wasn't looking.

A historical figure. A country. An institution. A family story. A belief you inherited before you were old enough to examine it. Often nothing about the object itself has changed. What changed is what you know about it. One fact arrives, then another, and something that once seemed perfectly smooth develops edges you hadn't noticed before. This can be exhilarating. It can also be disorienting, with the ground shifting slightly underneath something you had built other beliefs on top of.

Human beings need familiarity to function at all. Otherwise ordinary life would be exhausting. Imagine waking each morning and having to establish from first principles that this is your bedroom, that person is your friend, gravity still holds and the object beside the bed is called a lamp.

Familiarity is a kind of cognitive shorthand. It lets us live inside the world rather than endlessly interrogating it. But shorthand has a cost. Once something has acquired the feeling of of course, we tend to stop asking what, exactly, the course was, who laid it, or whether it still leads where we think it does.

Door

Look at the word again.

Door.

It has probably recovered its meaning by now. Four letters. One syllable. Perfectly ordinary.

But a door is also a boundary. It divides here from there, known from unknown, inside from outside. Open one and the unfamiliar walks in. Walk through one and the familiar world is suddenly, quietly, behind you.

This is older ground than cognitive science. The Romans had a two-faced god of doors and gates, Janus, who protected both entrance and exit. But the symbolic life of the threshold extends far beyond Rome. Across cultures, doors and gates have marked the boundary between one state and another, between the ordinary and the sacred, between inner and outer worlds, and even between life and death.

Some of the rituals surrounding thresholds are remarkably physical. Shoes are removed before crossing certain entrances. Brides have been carried over thresholds. In the folklore of the British Isles, doors were sometimes opened when someone was dying to ease the passage of the soul. In Indonesia, house doors have been opened during childbirth to make the baby's emergence easier. Ancient Egyptian tombs contained symbolic doorways through which the soul might pass.

These customs are separated by enormous distances in place, time and belief. They do not prove that a doorway possesses some universal psychological meaning. What they show is something quieter and, to me, just as interesting. Human beings have repeatedly looked at the ordinary act of crossing from one space into another and found more in it than architecture.

Perhaps that is why the small experiment with a pen and a word is more interesting than it first appears. Repeat something familiar long enough and, for a genuine share of people, the automatic feeling of familiarity falters. For a few seconds, you are left looking directly at something you know as though you don't quite know it after all.

Researchers called that jamais vu.

There may be something worth borrowing from it deliberately and applying, not as a scientific technique, but as a habit of thought. Occasionally we might allow something we "know" to become strange enough to ask how we actually know it. Where did we learn it, and from whom? Have we examined it, or have we simply encountered it many times? What evidence would change our minds? Whose experience have we never encountered? What are we not being shown? What has become so familiar that we have stopped seeing it at all?

A door is still a door.

Until you look at it for long enough. Then, sometimes, it becomes an opening.
