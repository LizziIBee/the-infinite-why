Ordinary Astonishment

The World Your Brain Gives You

You never experience the machinery that constructs reality. You experience its conclusion.

By Lizzie I Bee • 14 August 2026

There is a peculiar fact about being alive that is almost impossible to notice.

Look away from these words for a moment.

Perhaps there is a wall in front of you, a window, a cup, a lamp, a patch of afternoon light. Whatever is there, it probably does not arrive as a puzzle. You do not consciously calculate edges or compare wavelengths. You do not decide which patches of colour belong together, estimate distances, identify objects and then assemble them into a room.

You simply look.

And there it is.

The world.

That apparent immediacy is one of the most convincing tricks the human brain performs, because the world you experience is not simply pouring through your eyes.

Before light ever becomes something you can see, it has already been filtered by the particular sensory equipment evolution happened to give our species. There are wavelengths other animals detect that we cannot. Birds can see into the ultraviolet. Elephants can hear frequencies below our hearing range. Every creature occupies what the biologist Jakob von Uexküll called an Umwelt: a perceptual world shaped by the senses and body of the animal experiencing it.

Even within our small human window onto reality, the information arriving at our senses does not uniquely specify what is out there.

Colour is a simple example. A red object does not carry a little packet of redness that enters the brain. The colour we perceive depends partly on the light falling on the object and on its surroundings. Change the illumination or the neighbouring colours and the same physical stimulus can look different.

Measure reflected light with an instrument and you get wavelengths.

Look at it with a human brain and you get red.

That difference fascinated the neuroscientist Dale Purves. Purves spent much of his career trying to understand how a brain confronted with ambiguous sensory information nevertheless manages to produce such a stable and useful world. His proposal was that perception depends heavily on accumulated experience. Faced with uncertain information, the nervous system draws on what similar patterns have meant in the past and produces the interpretation most likely to be useful now.

You don't experience the inference.

You experience the answer.

The invisible guess

There is now considerable evidence that prior knowledge influences what we perceive. Experiments using ambiguous images have even traced information flowing from higher visual areas back toward earlier visual cortex when perception agrees with a person's long-established expectations. Perception appears to involve traffic in both directions: sensory evidence travelling inward, and prior knowledge influencing its interpretation.

This is why visual illusions are scientifically useful rather than merely entertaining. An illusion is a place where the system's normally excellent assumptions encounter an unusual situation and become visible. Two identical lines can appear different in length when surrounding perspective cues suggest that one lies farther away. A static image can seem to move. A colour can appear to change when its surroundings change.

The brain has not suddenly stopped working. In a sense, it is doing exactly what it normally does. The unusual stimulus has simply exposed the assumptions that ordinarily help perception succeed.

And that changes the way we might think about seeing. The brain is not a camera recording a world and handing the recording to consciousness. Nor does this mean that reality is imaginary. There really is something outside us constraining what we perceive. Walk into a closed door because you have decided it is open, and reality will correct the theory rather abruptly.

The subtler point is that experience and external reality are not the same thing. Our experience is the biological solution to the problem of navigating reality. And evolution did not need to give us every fact about the universe. It needed to give organisms enough useful information to survive and act.

Donald Hoffman takes that idea much further. His "interface theory of perception" argues that natural selection should favour perceptions tuned to biological fitness rather than perceptions that faithfully reproduce the objective structure of reality. He compares perception to the icons on a computer desktop, useful representations that allow us to act without showing us the complicated machinery underneath.

It is a provocative theory, not a settled description of how perception works, and its stronger claims remain controversial. But the metaphor reveals something important even without accepting Hoffman's conclusions. When you drag a document into a folder on a computer, you do not imagine that a tiny sheet of paper has physically travelled into a blue cardboard folder somewhere inside the machine. The icon is useful precisely because it hides the machinery.

Our perceptions may differ enormously from computer icons, but they too spare us the machinery. We experience surfaces, objects, sounds, distances, colours.

Not neural computations.

Not electrical activity.

Not probabilities.

Not photons.

A world.

The brain without a conductor

There is another assumption hiding inside that picture. If the brain constructs our experience, perhaps somewhere inside it there must be something doing the constructing.

A command centre.

A final processor.

Some neural headquarters where everything comes together.

Neuroscience has spent more than a century mapping functions onto brain structures, and localisation has produced extraordinary discoveries. Damage to particular areas can cause remarkably specific deficits. Different regions plainly are specialised in important ways.

But Luiz Pessoa argues that the familiar map can also mislead us if we begin imagining the brain as a collection of independent modules, each performing its assigned task. His alternative is what he calls the entangled brain, functions arising from overlapping, distributed networks whose components continually interact, a framework based on complexity and emergence rather than a simple chain of isolated processors.

Pessoa reaches for an unlikely image from the natural world to explain it.

A murmuration of starlings.

Thousands of birds can turn almost simultaneously, forming enormous shapes that contract, stretch and roll across the sky. Yet there is no lead starling drawing the shape. No bird knows what the whole flock looks like. Each responds to local information and nearby birds. From those interactions something appears that exists at the level of the group: the murmuration.

Emergence occurs elsewhere too. No individual speaker invented the English language. No single trader contains a market price. Complex patterns can arise from interactions among components even though none of the components possesses the pattern itself.

Perhaps some of our questions about the brain have been distorted by our desire to find the bird responsible for the murmuration.

Where is fear?

Where is memory?

Where is the self?

Where is consciousness?

Sometimes there are meaningful anatomical answers to questions like these. But increasingly, there are also answers involving relationships, networks and changing patterns of activity.

And then consciousness produces an even stranger problem. What happens when there is nothing to experience?

Imagine somebody interrupting you unexpectedly and asking: what were you just thinking about?

Sometimes there is an answer. Dinner. A conversation. Something you forgot to do. The sentence you're reading.

And sometimes, strangely, there seems to be nothing.

The neuroscientist Thomas Andrillon and colleagues study these episodes under the wonderfully literal name mind blanking. In experience-sampling experiments, participants performing a sustained task are interrupted and asked what was happening in their minds. When "thinking about nothing" is offered alongside alternatives such as focusing on the task or mind-wandering, participants select the blank option about 15 per cent of the time.

That could easily be dismissed as unreliable introspection. Except the blanks seem to leave traces. People are slower to respond when reporting them. Brain imaging has found an unusually widespread pattern of connectivity during mind blanking. Other work has found physiological changes associated with lower arousal and slow brain waves resembling those seen around sleep. The emerging possibility is that some spontaneous mind blanks may represent brief, sleep-like interruptions intruding into wakefulness.

The research is young, and Andrillon is careful about what remains unknown. But mind blanking raises an unexpectedly profound question.

For a long time, consciousness research has distinguished between being conscious and being conscious of something. You can lose consciousness under anaesthesia. And while awake, you can be conscious of a bird outside the window without being conscious of countless other things happening around and inside you.

Mind blanking introduces an awkward third possibility. What if you can be conscious while apparently having no particular conscious content at all?

The stream of consciousness may not be quite the uninterrupted stream we imagine it to be. And once that distinction appears, the puzzle becomes much larger.

The part we still cannot explain

Neuroscience can investigate which brain processes accompany conscious perception. It can compare activity when a stimulus reaches awareness with activity when it does not. It can investigate what changes during sleep, anaesthesia and brain injury. It can test competing models of how information becomes available to conscious experience.

And researchers are very much still arguing about the answer.

In a major preregistered collaboration published in Nature in 2025, researchers deliberately pitted predictions from two prominent theories against one another: Global Neuronal Workspace Theory and Integrated Information Theory. The study involved 256 participants and used fMRI, magnetoencephalography and intracranial recordings. The proponents of each theory agreed in advance on what would count as evidence against their own position, before a single result came in. The findings challenged important predictions of both theories rather than producing the long-awaited winner.

That matters because beneath the experiments lies a question that has stubbornly survived enormous scientific progress.

We can increasingly describe what the brain does. But why should any of it feel like anything?

Why isn't colour processing simply colour processing? Why is there redness? Why isn't the detection of tissue damage simply a biological signal initiating avoidance? Why does pain hurt?

This is related to what the philosopher David Chalmers famously called the hard problem of consciousness: explaining why physical information processing should be accompanied by subjective experience at all. Not everyone accepts that this constitutes a separate or insoluble problem, and many neuroscientists deliberately concentrate on questions that can be attacked experimentally.

Others think the difficulty points towards something more fundamental. The philosopher Philip Goff is among contemporary defenders of panpsychism, the idea that consciousness, or some extremely rudimentary precursor of it, may be a fundamental feature of reality rather than something that appears from entirely non-conscious matter once brains become sufficiently complicated.

That does not mean rocks have thoughts. Nor has neuroscience discovered that atoms are conscious. Panpsychism is a philosophical position, and it comes with formidable problems of its own, particularly explaining how hypothetical tiny forms of experience could combine into the unified consciousness of a person.

Other proposals go further still. Hoffman has developed a theory in which conscious agents, rather than spacetime and physical objects as we perceive them, occupy the fundamental level of reality. And researchers on the fringes of mainstream consciousness science have proposed other possibilities, including forms of non-local consciousness.

These ideas should not be placed on the same evidentiary footing as experiments on visual perception or measurements of brain activity. But their existence tells us something.

We have not solved the problem.

The world that appears

And so we return to the room.

Nothing about it looks mysterious. There is the table. There is the window. There is the particular colour of the wall. Perhaps there is a sound somewhere outside.

It all feels immediate because almost everything required to make it feel that way has been hidden from you. Your sensory organs have already excluded most of the physical information available in the environment. Your nervous system has transformed what remains. Previous experience has influenced its interpretation. Distributed neural systems have interacted across a brain that is itself inseparable from the body it developed with and the environment it must navigate.

And somehow the result is not experienced as inference.

It is experienced as being here.

That may be the strangest part. We spend our lives inside a constructed experience so convincing that we ordinarily mistake the experience for the construction site. The machinery disappears. The guess disappears. The networks disappear. Even, sometimes, the thoughts disappear.

And still there is this extraordinary fact: something is happening, and there is something it is like to be here while it happens.

For all that neuroscience has revealed about the brain, that ordinary fact remains one of the deepest unanswered questions we have.

Perhaps the astonishing thing is not that our senses sometimes deceive us.

It is that from a narrow stream of incomplete information, an organ locked inside a dark skull gives us a world at all.
