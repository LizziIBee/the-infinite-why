Proof of Hope

The exam designed by the students who needed it

How a question raised by refugee teenagers in a Beirut camp became a qualification now being developed and scaled with Cambridge.

By Lizzie I. Bee • 12 August 2026

For displaced young people, education can fail at an oddly bureaucratic final hurdle.

Someone may have spent years learning through refugee schools, NGOs, different countries or self-study, and be genuinely ready for university. But if none of that adds up to a single, recognisable certificate, universities and employers are left trying to interpret an educational history that simply doesn't fit their forms.

Not a lack of ability. A lack of paperwork.

The question the students asked

At the Alsama Project, which runs education centres for refugee and displaced teenagers in Beirut's Shatila camp, that gap wasn't identified by a policy paper or a donor report.

It was raised by the students themselves.

In 2023, after years of working to become university-ready, Alsama's own students asked the organisation a simple, difficult question: how were they supposed to prove what they could do?

There wasn't a good answer. So they built one.

An exam built around capability, not geography

The result became known as the G12++.

Rather than requiring students to have followed one country's curriculum without interruption, the qualification was designed around what displacement actually does to an education: it interrupts it, scatters it, and moves it across borders and school systems. So the G12++ assesses critical thinking, communication, problem-solving and academic potential instead, in a form intended to make sense regardless of where or how someone learned.

It costs around US$20 to sit, a price built specifically so it could be covered by NGOs working in refugee camps and other underserved places, wherever a computer and an internet connection can be found.

It was field-tested across six countries with 183 students. The results showed Alsama students achieving the same average scores as formally educated students at international schools, while post-beta psychometric analysis supported the exam's quality.

What has already happened

This has moved well beyond a hopeful proposal.

The first live G12++ examination took place in Shatila in February 2026. The qualification has since been independently benchmarked by Ecctis at UK RQF Level 2 up to Level 3 for benchmarkable subjects. In May, Cambridge University Press & Assessment announced a formal partnership with Alsama to help strengthen and scale the qualification internationally, signed at the Education World Forum in London. Cambridge has said the qualification was originally inspired by Alsama's own students.

The first certified graduates are expected to move into university, vocational training and employment from September 2026.

Alsama's longer-term ambition is striking: by 2040, they want 50,000 displaced young people a year to be able to earn the G12++.

For context, around nine in every hundred refugees worldwide currently make it into higher education, against roughly forty-two in every hundred among the general global population. That gap is not, for the most part, a gap in ability. It is a gap in recognition.

Why this story matters

For years, the instinct in refugee education has largely been to help displaced children fit back into the system that existed before their lives were interrupted: catch them up, slot them in, hope the gap closes.

Alsama's students asked a different question. Not how do we get back into the system built for uninterrupted lives? but why does that have to be the only system there is?

That is a genuinely different kind of hope from the kind that arrives as charity. It doesn't ask people to be grateful for what they're given. It asks whether the obstacle everyone had quietly accepted as inevitable needed to exist in the first place.

Sometimes it turns out it didn't.

A thought for today

We tend to assume that systems are fixed, and that people have to bend themselves to fit them.

The G12++ suggests something less obvious. Sometimes the system was never as fixed as it looked. It was simply built by people who never had to imagine a childhood interrupted by war, and so never thought to design around one.

It took students who had lived that interruption to see what was missing. And it took people willing to listen to them, rather than speak for them, to help build the thing that had been missing all along.

There is something worth sitting with in that. The people closest to a problem are so often the ones best placed to solve it, if anyone thinks to ask them.
